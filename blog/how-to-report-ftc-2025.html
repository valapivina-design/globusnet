How to Report Fraud to the FTC in 2025 – Complete Step-by-Step Guide

The Federal Trade Commission processed over 2.6 million fraud reports in 2023. Here is exactly how to submit yours — and how to make it count.

Before You Start: Gather Your Evidence

A strong FTC report includes specific, detailed information. Before going to ReportFraud.ftc.gov, collect:

  • The scammer's name, username, email, phone number, or website
  • Screenshots of all communications
  • Payment confirmation numbers, bank reference numbers, or crypto transaction IDs
  • Dates and amounts for every payment made
  • A brief written timeline of what happened

Step 1: Go to ReportFraud.ftc.gov

Navigate directly to ReportFraud.ftc.gov — the FTC's official reporting portal. Do not use third-party "fraud reporting services" that claim to submit on your behalf. The FTC portal is free and direct.

Step 2: Select Your Fraud Category

The FTC system categorizes fraud types to route your report appropriately. The main categories include: Online Shopping, Imposter Scams, Investment Fraud, Identity Theft, Romance Scams, Business Opportunities, and more. Choose the most accurate fit — this determines which enforcement teams receive your report.

Step 3: Describe What Happened

The narrative section is critical. Be specific: what did the scammer say, what did they promise, how did you pay, and what happened when you tried to get your money back. Vague reports are less actionable. Use specific dates, dollar amounts, and quotes from communications where relevant.

Step 4: Provide Scammer Details

Fill in everything you know about the scammer — every name used, every phone number, email address, website URL, and payment account. Even partial information is valuable. The FTC uses this to connect reports from multiple victims of the same operation.

Step 5: Submit and Save Your Confirmation

After submitting, you'll receive a confirmation number by email. Save this. The FTC may use it to follow up, and you'll need it if you reference your report with other agencies like the FBI's IC3 or your state attorney general.

What Happens After You Report to the FTC?

Your report enters the FTC's Consumer Sentinel Network — a database shared with over 3,000 law enforcement partners. The FTC uses aggregated reports to identify fraud patterns and launch enforcement actions. Individual cases are rarely investigated directly, but your report contributes to pattern-based enforcement that has resulted in hundreds of millions in consumer refunds.

What the FTC Cannot Do

The FTC cannot: investigate your individual case, recover your money directly, or prosecute individual fraudsters. For individual case investigation, you need to supplement your FTC report with additional steps — including your bank, the FBI IC3, and potentially a private investigator for complex cases.

Additional US Fraud Reporting — File All That Apply

  • FBI IC3 (ic3.gov) — especially for internet-based crime and crypto fraud
  • SEC (sec.gov/tcr) — for investment fraud and securities violations
  • CFTC (cftc.gov/complaint) — for forex, commodities, and crypto commodity fraud
  • Your state attorney general — many states have active fraud units
  • Your bank — report separately and request a transaction recall if applicable

📌 File Multiple Reports

Don't assume reporting to the FTC covers everything. For significant losses, file with the FTC and the FBI IC3 at minimum. If investment fraud is involved, also file with the SEC and CFTC. Multiple reports increase the chance of coordinated action.
